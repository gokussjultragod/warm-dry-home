It is one of the most argued-about questions in British households every winter. The answer from energy experts is fairly clear, but there are exceptions worth knowing.

Short answer: for most homes with a gas boiler and radiators, it is cheaper to heat only when you need it, using a timer and thermostat, than to keep it on low all day. A home loses heat all the time it is warmer than outside, so the longer it is kept warm, the more energy it uses. The main exception is a heat pump, which usually works best running steadily at a low temperature.

Why "on low all day" usually costs more

Heat leaks out of a house through walls, windows and the roof at a rate that depends on how much warmer inside is than outside. Keep it warm for 24 hours and it leaks heat for 24 hours. Heat it for 6 hours and it leaks much less. The extra energy needed to warm the house back up is smaller than the energy saved while the heating was off.

A simple way to picture it

Think of your house as a bucket with a hole in it, and heat as the water. The warmer you keep the house, the faster heat pours out of the hole. Keeping the bucket topped up all day means you are paying for everything that leaks out all day. Letting the level drop while you are out, then filling it back up before you get home, loses less in total.

How much you save depends on how well insulated the home is. In a draughty, poorly insulated house the saving from using a timer is bigger, because heat escapes faster. In a very well insulated new-build the difference is smaller, but a timer still usually wins.

Gas on the Ofgem price cap costs about 8p per kWh from October to December 2026, against about 26p for electricity. That is why heating with a gas boiler on a timer is usually cheaper than keeping plug-in electric heaters running.

When steady heating makes sense

  • Heat pumps: they are most efficient at low flow temperatures, so long, gentle running is normal. Follow your installer's advice.
  • Someone is home all day, for example working from home or caring for a baby or older relative. Then you need heat all day anyway; the question becomes which rooms.
  • Damp-prone homes: letting rooms go very cold for long periods raises humidity and condensation. A modest minimum temperature can prevent mould. See stopping condensation.

Settings that save money

  • Set the main thermostat to the lowest comfortable temperature, often 18 to 20°C. Each degree lower saves a noticeable share of heating costs.
  • Use timer slots for mornings and evenings, starting about 30 minutes before you need the warmth.
  • Turn radiator valves down in rooms you don't use, but not fully off in rooms prone to damp.
  • Lower the boiler flow temperature on a combi boiler, following official guidance for your model.
  • Warm the person, not the house, for short spells: a heated throw or electric blanket costs pennies per hour.
